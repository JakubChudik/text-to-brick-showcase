Brick by Proof

A sentence goes in; a LEGO model comes out that a person can actually build: every part exists in its colour, every connection is checked, it stands on a table, and it comes with a parts list and step-by-step instructions. This page explains how we get there, and why the process looks the way it does.

What we add

Buildable, proven

A language model can describe a castle; it cannot tell you whether brick 4,812 floats. Our engine places real LDraw parts on a grid, checks connections, colour availability and collisions, and solves the model for strength before calling it buildable.

Taste that compounds

Every blind pick and every comment from the owner becomes a written taste rule, a lesson, or an automated check. The next build starts where the last one ended, instead of from scratch.

Scenes, not objects

A set is a big scene made of a few smaller scenes, each with a dominant figure, and many small micro-scenes to discover: a gløgg stall, a lamplighter on his ladder, Bilbo with his pipe.

Ready to build

Each finished set has a parts list by part and colour, a readable instruction booklet, the model file for BrickLink Studio, and calibrated renders where colours look like real plastic.

The process

The owner's eye is the scarce resource, so the process spends it on small pieces that are easy to judge, and spends the machine's time on everything that can be checked automatically.

The red loops are cheap: a failed check costs seconds, a redo touches one piece. The green loop is how the next set starts better than this one.

Why this way, and what we tried first

Most of the current process exists because an earlier version failed in a way we measured. 43 short, timed experiments over two days changed one thing at a time and kept what the numbers supported.

ChoiceWhat we tried firstWhat we do now

How we judge a build

No single number says a model is good. We measure it in layers, from "can it exist" to "does the owner love it". The automated measures are calibrated against official LEGO sets (148–156 models from the LDraw library), so "good" means "within the range real sets fall in", not a guess.

LayerWhat we measureHow
VerdictDoes the owner prefer it?Blind X/Y picks against Claude Opus in BrickAgent and against our previous version (a new version must not lose what won). Keep / redo / maybe on each piece. A sealed critic writes its pick before the owner does; it agreed on 82% of past pairs, and is reported, never used as a gate.
BuildableCan someone actually build it?Every part exists in its colour; studs and connections mate; nothing collides or floats; it stands on a table (a strength solve with a margin); every instruction step can be placed in order; an independent checker (BrickAgent) agrees.
Reads at a glanceDo the things that matter show up?Each must-read feature (a face, a sign, the dominant figure) is measured in the review camera: size in pixels, how much is hidden, which way it faces, contrast against its neighbours, proportion to the whole. Every micro-scene must be seen by its camera; each smaller scene's dominant must be the largest thing in it.
Looks like LEGOIs it shaped and coloured the way real sets are?A colour count kept within what official sets of the same size use (no "rainbow" models); calm, consistent palettes; slopes and curves covering silhouettes rather than bare stairs; touching structures that differ in colour or part family; enough special parts instead of plain bricks.
Fun to buildIs building it interesting from bag to bag?Build entropy per section against official-set bands: variety of part types, colours, lengths and techniques, how often a step brings something new, and how long it stays monotonous. Every bag should end in a reveal. The booklet must show each step's new parts clearly (at least 4–6% of the picture).
Novel and surprisingDoes it use parts cleverly?Parts used in roles beyond their usual one, learned from official sets (a fez as a tree top, a claw as a palm frond, aeroplane wings as fir boughs). Diversity within a kind: no two ents, trees or fire pits the same.
Alive and specificIs it this subject, and is something happening?The owner's taste rules, 22 so far, each traced to a verdict: integrated scenes rather than objects on a plate; depth instead of a theatre box; a story moment; recognisable sculptures; creatures with their defining traits; a lot happening; a city's at-first-sight giveaways; snow that does not merge ground and roofs.
ProcessAre we getting better and faster?Build loops to an acceptable result per kind of thing; how many mistakes the 3-second checks catch before building; the share of findings that were knowable in advance; time per loop; cost per winning build; how true the render colours are.
